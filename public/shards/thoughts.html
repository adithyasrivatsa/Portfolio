10:00 AM — the mood

I started thinking about religion at 10 AM today. Which is probably not the most productive thing to do at 10 AM. But anyway.

I don't think Abrahamic religions are necessarily stupid. That's too easy. They have genuinely strong philosophical arguments. The problem is that when I follow some of those arguments to their logical conclusions, I sometimes just sit there thinking:

Bro... what?
10:04 AM — Christianity

Free will, foreknowledge, and infinite DLCThe judgment problem

God gives humans free will, knows exactly what they're going to do, knows their entire circumstances, and then judges them for the decisions they make.

I didn't sign up for this. If you gave me free will, why am I being punished for using it?

And then Jesus comes along preaching forgiveness, compassion, and standing up for people society considers sinners. Sometimes the story almost feels like Jesus is the guy saying "Maybe stop being so damn harsh on these people." Which creates a weird philosophical tension.

Then there's hell. A finite human being commits finite actions during a finite lifetime, and somehow the punishment can become infinite. I'm not convinced that's justice.

It sounds more like revenge with infinite DLC.

10:11 AM — Islam

Discipline, determinism, and the revelation problemBecome an author at this point

I'll actually give Islam a lot of respect for its discipline. The devotion is impressive. Prayer, fasting, religious practice, community structure — there's an insane level of consistency there.

But then I run into the same philosophical problem. If God knows everything and has ultimate control over everything, where exactly does human free will fit?

And then comes the revelation problem. How many prophets and books do you need? If God is an omniscient being trying to communicate something important to humanity, why does the message require centuries of interpretation, competing schools, theological arguments, and people fighting over what the author meant?

Bro, become an author at this point.

And apparently not a very good one. Because if the consequences of misunderstanding the message can be eternal, I'd expect the instructions to be crystal fucking clear.

"Dear Adithya, considering your cognition, upbringing, environment and intellectual limitations, here is exactly what I meant." — the personalized book I'd have written, if I were God.

Done. No theological civil wars.

10:19 AM — Hinduism

An ancient philosophical MCUAdmirable and completely insane

The sheer amount of philosophy is ridiculous. You've got Vedanta, Samkhya, Yoga, Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Mimamsa, Shaiva traditions, Vaishnava traditions, Charvaka, and a ridiculous number of other schools.

It's basically an ancient philosophical MCU. You could probably spend your entire life studying it and still die with three hundred unexplored side quests.

But what I find genuinely fascinating is that Hindu philosophical traditions sometimes question themselves. Take the Nasadiya Sukta — it asks whether we can even know how creation happened. Who knows? Maybe even the highest being doesn't know. And I'm like:

Now we're talking.

You've got skepticism, agnosticism, atheistic philosophies, theism, non-dualism, dualism — all existing within this enormous intellectual ecosystem. It feels like ancient humans were having existential crises long before we had Wi-Fi. And that's something I actually admire. They didn't always pretend they had one clean answer.

But then somehow, after all this questioning, we still ended up with people saying: "Yeah anyway, God definitely exists." So I'm still confused. But perhaps that's the point. Maybe religion was never supposed to be a single deadline.

a short aside on morality
10:27 AM — a working theory

Inherited, not perfectReading the book without worshipping every page

To me, religion makes more sense as something inherited. It's basically our ancestors passing down a giant book containing their ideas about morality, justice, suffering, death, consciousness, nature, and existence.

Some of it is brilliant. Some of it is stupid. Some of it was incredibly useful for the society that created it. Some of it probably made absolutely no sense. But it's still part of the intellectual heritage of humanity — our ancestors saying "This is how we tried to understand the world." And I think we should be allowed to read that book without being forced to treat every page as perfect.

Because morality itself should probably keep evolving. I increasingly believe rehabilitation is more meaningful than simply punishing someone. A person doesn't exist independently from society — their upbringing, environment, education, poverty, family, culture, experiences, and the people around them all shape them. When one person fails, sometimes society has failed somewhere too.

That doesn't mean people shouldn't face consequences. Someone who is dangerous may need to be restrained to protect others. But punishment purely for the sake of making someone suffer? I'm not convinced. If we can turn someone who harmed society into someone who contributes to it, why wouldn't we? That seems like a better form of justice.

If our understanding of justice improves, our concept of justice should improve with it.

10:34 AM — Buddhism

"Cool culture you've got here."Everyone wants Buddha on their team

This is where I started finding the whole thing even more interesting. Buddhism might be one of the most interesting examples of how religion and culture can actually coexist.

Buddhism basically spawned somewhere and then travelled across Asia going "Cool culture you've got here."

  • China? Buddhism gets intertwined with Daoist and Confucian traditions.
  • Japan? Buddhism and Shinto become deeply intertwined.
  • Tibet? It develops into something completely its own.

And across Asia, Buddhism repeatedly adapted to the cultures it entered. And then comes the funniest part:

Everyone wants Buddha on their team.

Different cultures interpreted him differently. Hindus incorporated Buddha into their own religious worldview. East Asian traditions incorporated Buddhist ideas alongside existing traditions. People didn't necessarily have to completely delete their previous cultural identity to engage with Buddhism.

And that, to me, is important. Because maybe religious harmony isn't about making everyone believe the same thing. Maybe it's about making religion feel like an option rather than a replacement.

10:42 AM — on expansion and loss

"Your gods aren't real."Culture disappearing feels different than losing an argument

That's where I think some people become uncomfortable with Abrahamic religions. And I don't mean every individual Christian, Muslim or Jew — obviously not. I'm talking about the historical experience of religious expansion.

Imagine your ancestors have followed a particular religion for thousands of years. Your festivals, stories, temples, gods, rituals, music, identity and understanding of the universe are all connected to it. Then another religion arrives and tells you "Actually, your gods aren't real." Of course you're going to feel threatened.

And historically, many pre-Abrahamic religious systems declined or disappeared as Christianity and Islam expanded — Kemetism, ancient Greek religion, Norse religion, many Celtic traditions faded; Persian religious traditions were heavily transformed.

Obviously, history isn't simply "Abrahamic religion arrived → old religion deleted." Politics, conquest, state power, economics, assimilation, persecution, demographic change and cultural exchange all played roles. But the result still matters.

When your ancestral worldview disappears, you don't experience that as an abstract theological debate. You experience it as your culture disappearing. And I think that's one reason some religious communities instinctively distance themselves from Abrahamic religions — not because they hate the people, but because they're protecting the feeling that "our way of understanding the world deserves to exist too."

That's why I think secularism alone doesn't automatically solve religious tension. Legal secularism matters — freedom of religion and freedom from religion. But simply saying "everyone is secular now" doesn't address the deeper cultural question. People don't just inherit beliefs. They inherit civilizations.

And maybe that's why Buddhism is so interesting: it shows religious interaction doesn't have to be zero-sum. Cultures can mix. Ideas can migrate. People can borrow. Religions can influence each other. And somehow, nobody has to completely disappear.

Not "you are wrong, therefore you must eventually become like me" — but "you believe something different. Cool. Let's coexist."

I don't think religion should be a competition where somebody has to win. I think it should be a collection of different attempts humanity has made to understand existence — and maybe nobody needs to agree on the final answer, because maybe there isn't one available to us yet.

10:51 AM — science, philosophy, religion

Newton did not have a "philosophy isn't my department" faceHow we accidentally split one thing into tribes

Come on, guys. Newton was literally doing natural philosophy. The man wasn't sitting there thinking "I'm a scientist. Philosophy isn't my department." For most of history, what we now call science was basically natural philosophy.

The separation became necessary because knowledge got ridiculously complicated — you can't realistically expect one person to master quantum mechanics, biology, ethics, metaphysics, mathematics and political philosophy. Specialization makes sense. But sometimes I think we've taken it too far.

Science can tell us what happens. Philosophy asks what it means. Science can tell us what we can do. Philosophy asks what we should do. Religion carries inherited moral and existential ideas. Science tests our claims about reality. Philosophy questions the assumptions behind both. These things don't need to be enemies.

Yet somehow we've turned them into tribes — religion versus science, science versus philosophy, religion versus religion, nation versus nation — everyone trying to prove that their framework completely defeated everyone else's. I don't think that's how human knowledge is supposed to work.

Religion gives us inherited wisdom. Philosophy interrogates it. Science tests our assumptions against reality. Society learns from the results. And then the next generation does the same thing — not because our ancestors were stupid, not because they were always right, but because they were trying. They passed down both their intelligence and their stupidity, their greatest insights and their biggest mistakes.

Perhaps our responsibility isn't to blindly preserve either. It's to figure out which parts deserve to survive.

Maybe religion shouldn't be a final answer. Maybe it should be a conversation — one that started thousands of years ago and hasn't ended yet.

And honestly? I'm perfectly fine with not knowing the final answer. Because if humanity ever becomes completely certain that it has understood everything about existence — that's probably when we should start worrying.
